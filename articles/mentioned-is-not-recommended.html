Mentioned Is Not Recommended

Most businesses stop reading an AI answer the moment their name shows up. That is the mistake. Inside the same answer, a brand can be picked, listed, or named to be set aside, and only one of those turns into a sale.

Ask an AI assistant which product to buy or service to use, and it will name several options. Most businesses stop reading there. If their name shows up, they assume they are in the running.

That assumption is where the money leaks. Being named and being recommended are two different events inside the same answer, and they do not track each other nearly as closely as people think.

Three different ways a name can appear

When we code audit answers, a brand can show up in an answer in three very different ways.

First, it can be picked. The answer puts it forward as a choice for the person asking, sometimes with a condition attached, like this one if you sell physical goods, that one if content is the product. That is a recommendation, and it is the only version that reliably turns into a sale.

Second, it can be listed. The answer names it in a price table, a feature comparison, or a rundown of who exists in the category, and recommends nobody. In our Shopify sample, the monthly cost question produced exactly this on all three engines. Twelve answers, price tables in every one, and zero brands picked in any of them.

Third, it can be named to be set aside. The answer mentions it so it can warn against it, price it out of the running, or describe it as something buyers outgrow. That still counts as a mention in most simple tracking tools. To a buyer, it is the opposite of a recommendation.

If your reporting lumps all three together into one mention count, your best-looking number may be hiding your worst problem.

What the gap looked like for the strongest brand we have tested

Shopify is the biggest name in e-commerce, so it is a useful stress test. If the gap between named and picked shows up for Shopify, it shows up for everyone.

In the open, unbranded questions in our sample, Shopify was named in 100 percent of answers on ChatGPT, Claude, and Gemini. Perfect visibility, by the simple measure.

The pick rate was lower. Shopify was shortlisted in 92 percent of ChatGPT answers, 87 percent of Gemini answers, and 92 percent of Claude answers. Strong numbers, and the cost question explains part of the gap on its own, because nobody gets picked there, so 92 percent was effectively the ceiling on this panel. But the direction matters more than the size. Even for the category leader, named in every answer did not mean picked in every answer.

The gap got sharper when we looked at share of attention. On Claude, Shopify took 22 percent of all platform mentions, exactly tied with WooCommerce, even though both were shortlisted at high rates. On ChatGPT Shopify held 30 percent of mentions outright, and on Gemini 27 percent. Same brand, same questions, three different stories depending on which engine a buyer happened to ask.

Why a mention count will mislead you

A mention count answers the easiest question, not the useful one. It tells you the AI knows you exist. It does not tell you whether the AI would hand you a buyer who is ready to choose.

It also hides the engine problem. A business that checks one assistant, sees its name, and stops, has learned what one reviewer thinks. Our sample kept finding that the engines disagree, sometimes on who wins, sometimes on whether anyone gets recommended at all. A single check, on a single engine, on a single day, is a snapshot of one opinion.

And it hides the question problem. A brand can be the default pick for starting out and still lose the one question its best buyers ask, on price, on a feature, on a use case. Category averages smooth that loss away. Buyers do not buy on category averages. They ask one specific question and take the shortlist it gives them.

What to measure instead

Three numbers, kept separate, will tell you more than any single visibility score.

One, the pick rate on open questions. Out of the answers where a buyer names no brand, how often does the answer actually put you forward as a choice? That is your recommendation rate, and it is the headline number in every audit we run.

Two, your pick rate by engine. If you are strong on ChatGPT and weak on Gemini, that is not a visibility problem in general. It is a specific gap, with specific reasons in the answers, and it can be worked on specifically.

Three, your pick rate by question. Find the buying questions where you are named but not picked, or not named at all, and read what the answer says instead. The reasons are usually in plain language in the answer itself, and they are usually things a business can address, in its content, its pricing page, or how it describes what it does.

How we ran this, and the limits

The figures here come from our Shopify sample audit. Twenty prompts, three engines (ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini via their APIs), four runs each, 240 answers. Thirteen prompts were unbranded and carry the headline figures. A brand counts as shortlisted when the answer presents it as a pick for the asker's situation, including conditional picks. Price table listings and avoid framings do not count.

Two limits, stated plainly. First, API answers are not identical to what a buyer sees in the consumer apps. They are the closest controlled way to run the same question many times, and every report we write says this. Second, 52 unbranded answers per engine is a structured sample, not the universe. Treat the rates as directional.
